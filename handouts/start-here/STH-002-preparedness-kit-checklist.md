<p class="lede">Build a portable go-bag for leaving quickly, then build a separate two-week supply for staying at home. A full two-week home supply is not meant to be carried during an evacuation.</p>

<div class="warning warning--caution"><strong>Local direction comes first:</strong> hazards, climate, water availability, and evacuation conditions vary. Follow local emergency-management and public-health authorities whenever their current instructions or recommended quantities differ from this checklist.</div>

Water is the minimum priority: CDC and the American Red Cross recommend storing at least **1 gallon per person per day**; the Red Cross lists a **two-week supply for home** and a **three-day supply for evacuation**. Your local authority may set a different evacuation amount.

## 2. Pack the portable go-bag separately

Pack only what each person can safely move, or arrange help. Do **not** delay an evacuation to collect supplies.

<div class="checklist"><ul>
<li>Water and ready-to-eat food for the evacuation period recommended by local authorities—not the entire two-week home supply</li>
<li>Essential medicines, copies of prescriptions, medical and accessibility supplies, and a list of health needs</li>
<li>Phone, charging cable, power bank, battery/hand-crank radio, flashlight or headlamp, and spare batteries</li>
<li>Identification and protected copies of contacts, plans, insurance, and other essential records</li>
<li>Small bills, keys, local map, basic first aid, hygiene items, masks, sturdy shoes, and climate layers</li>
<li>For children and animals: essential food, water, medicines, comfort items, identification, and safe restraints</li>
</ul></div>

Label bags. Keep medical, communication, mobility, sensory, and animal items beside the person or animal who needs them. Review transport, shelter, and backup-power arrangements with support networks before an emergency.

## 3. Choose food and supplies people can use

<div class="checklist"><ul>
<li>Choose shelf-stable foods that require little water, refrigeration, or cooking. Include a manual can opener and utensils.</li>
<li>Plan for allergies, intolerances, religious or cultural diets, diabetes or other medical diets, infant feeding, and texture or feeding assistance. Check every label; do not assume an emergency food is safe.</li>
<li>Include glasses, hearing-aid batteries, communication boards, mobility-device parts, chargers, adaptive utensils, service-animal supplies, and sensory or comfort items as applicable.</li>
<li>Ask a clinician or pharmacist how to obtain and safely store an emergency medication supply; never change doses or use expired medicine unless an authorized official specifically directs it.</li>
</ul></div>

## 4. Store safely and rotate

<div class="checklist"><ul>
<li>Keep supplies accessible, dry, and protected from pests, flooding, extreme temperatures, and household chemicals. Do not block an exit. Split heavy water and food among smaller containers or locations.</li>
<li>Use unopened commercially bottled water by its printed date. For water you fill yourself, use sanitized food-grade containers, label the date, keep them cool and out of direct sunlight, and replace the water every 6 months. Never use a container that held toxic chemicals.</li>
<li>Keep food in a cool, dry place in closed containers. Discard cans that are leaking, bulging, badly dented, or heavily rusted. When in doubt about food safety, throw it out; never taste food to test it.</li>
<li>Store medicines exactly as their labels or pharmacist direct, secured from children and animals. Keep batteries and power banks dry and at manufacturer-recommended temperatures; inspect for swelling, leaks, corrosion, or damage.</li>
<li>Every 6 months—and after using the kit—check water replacement dates, food and medicine dates, batteries and charge levels, document accuracy, clothing fit, animal needs, and seasonal hazards. Use and replace items before they expire when safe to do so.</li>
</ul></div>

## 5. Build it in stages

If buying everything now is not possible, start with what you already have. Do not take on unsafe debt or skip current medicines, food, rent, or utilities.

1. **This week:** pack evacuation essentials and three days of water, food, medicines, sanitation items, and lighting.
2. **Next:** add one day per person and animal at a time until the home supply reaches 14 days.
3. **Then:** add communications, backup power, documents, cash, climate, accessibility, and comfort items in the order most critical to your household.
4. **Keep it ready:** rotate supplies into normal use, replace them gradually, and revisit the plan when household needs or local guidance change.

Ask local emergency management, public health, utilities, schools, disability organizations, food assistance programs, animal services, and community groups about local guidance or assistance.
