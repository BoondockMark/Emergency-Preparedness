<p class="lede">Research the hazards that could affect the places you use, record the official instruction for each one, and recheck it regularly.</p>

<div class="warning"><strong>During an emergency, current instructions from local authorities take priority over this worksheet.</strong> General preparedness guidance helps you plan; it does not set local evacuation zones, routes, shelter locations, alert thresholds, or re-entry rules.</div>

## 2. Ask official sources

<div class="checklist"><ul>
<li><strong>Local emergency management:</strong> ask which hazards affect each place, who can order evacuation or sheltering, and where official zone and route information is published.</li>
<li><strong>Official alerting services:</strong> enroll every household member who can receive alerts; note coverage, languages, accessibility features, and a backup method. Wireless Emergency Alerts require no sign-up, but local opt-in systems may.</li>
<li><strong>Map owners:</strong> check official evacuation-zone maps and, where applicable, flood, fire, earthquake, tsunami, dam, landslide, severe-weather, or other hazard maps. Record the map issuer, layer, and date—not only a screenshot.</li>
<li><strong>Workplaces, schools, and care sites:</strong> request their emergency, reunification, closure, and transportation procedures.</li>
<li><strong>Utility providers:</strong> ask about hazard-specific shutoffs, outage alerts, safe service interruption, medical-equipment programs, and restoration information.</li>
<li><strong>Insurance and property records:</strong> review declarations, exclusions, deductibles, inventories, surveys, disclosures, permits, and assessor or recorder documents. Ask the insurer or record holder to explain unclear entries.</li>
</ul></div>

<p><strong>Map caution:</strong> a map may describe one hazard or planning purpose only. Do not treat an insurance, parcel, or risk map as an evacuation order. Confirm its purpose with the issuing authority.</p>

## Turn research into action

<div class="checklist"><ul>
<li>Compare hazards across home, work, school/care, and routes; identify alternatives where one event could affect several places.</li>
<li>Save official sources on phones and on paper. Keep a battery-radio or other backup method appropriate to local guidance.</li>
<li>Write zone names and triggers into the household plan; do not preselect a route that officials may close.</li>
<li>Discuss assistance, transport, medicines, power, communication, and animal needs before an alert.</li>
<li>Review after a move, school/job/route change, insurance renewal, map revision, major incident, or official guidance update—and at least annually.</li>
</ul></div>

## Binder follow-through

<p><strong>Section 7 — Hazard Guides:</strong> use the guide for each hazard you identify to learn protective actions and hazard-specific supplies. Confirm every locally controlled trigger, zone, and instruction with its issuing authority.</p>

<p><strong>Section 8 — Plans & Records:</strong> transfer verified alerts, contacts, meeting places, evacuation/shelter decisions, access needs, insurance information, inventories, and review dates into the household plan and protected records.</p>
