<p class="lede">Plan for every animal before an alert. Identify who will move each animal, what they need, where they can go, and what happens if normal transport is not possible.</p>

<div class="warning"><strong>Act early for livestock:</strong> loading and moving large animals takes time, trained handlers, suitable vehicles, and a confirmed destination. Follow current instructions from local emergency management, animal control, agricultural extension, and your veterinarian. Do not enter a threatened area or delay your own evacuation to move an animal.</div>

## Prepare every animal

<div class="checklist"><ul>
<li>Use permanent identification when appropriate; add a securely attached temporary contact label. Keep current photos showing the animal and its identifying features, including a photo with the owner or handler.</li>
<li>Protect copies of veterinary, vaccination, prescription, registration, microchip, ownership, and any health-certificate records. Keep veterinarian and poison-control contact information with them.</li>
<li>Pack current medications, written dosing and care instructions, and needed administration supplies. Ask the veterinarian how to manage medicines requiring refrigeration.</li>
<li>Rotate animal food and drinking water; pack bowls or feeders, a manual can opener if needed, and species-appropriate sanitation supplies.</li>
<li>Practice calm confinement and loading with the carrier, crate, leash, harness, halter, or other restraint the animal will actually use.</li>
<li>Name a primary and backup caregiver. Give each person written permission, access instructions, the animal inventory, and a way to reach the veterinarian.</li>
<li>Confirm more than one evacuation destination and route. Ask each destination what species it accepts, what records or equipment it requires, and whether owner and animal can remain together.</li>
</ul></div>

<p><strong>Do not assume a public shelter, hotel, boarding facility, fairground, or rescue site will accept an animal.</strong> Rules, capacity, opening times, and health requirements vary and can change during an incident. Verify them locally before leaving and again when officials activate evacuation or shelter sites.</p>

## Household pets

<div class="checklist"><ul>
<li>Assign one carrier or safe restraint to each pet; label it with animal and household contact information.</li>
<li>Pack litter and a pan, waste bags, cleaning supplies, familiar bedding, and species-specific heat, light, habitat, or handling equipment.</li>
<li>Identify pet-friendly lodging, boarding, friends or relatives, and any locally designated animal sheltering. Keep an option outside the immediate hazard area.</li>
<li>Plan for frightened or hiding pets: record likely hiding places and keep safe capture tools where they are accessible.</li>
</ul></div>

## Service animals

<p>A service animal is not a pet. Keep the animal with its handler whenever possible and include its work and access needs in the household plan.</p>

<div class="checklist"><ul>
<li>Pack food, water, medicines, sanitation supplies, a leash or harness, and any gear needed for the animal's work. Do not separate the animal from the handler's essential mobility, communication, medical, or power equipment.</li>
<li>Plan accessible transport with enough space for the handler, animal, mobility device, and supplies. Arrange an accessible backup vehicle and driver.</li>
<li>Record the animal's tasks, commands, routine, stress signals, and care instructions in case a backup caregiver must assist.</li>
<li>Verify current shelter and transport arrangements with local officials. Under the ADA, service animals generally accompany people with disabilities in public facilities; different rules may apply to animals that provide comfort but are not trained to perform a disability-related task.</li>
</ul></div>

## Livestock and other large animals

<div class="warning warning--caution"><strong>Use local, incident-specific direction:</strong> suitable routes, loading sites, movement controls, health documentation, shelter locations, and options for animals left in place depend on the hazard and jurisdiction. Generic advice cannot confirm that a road or facility is safe or open.</div>

<div class="checklist"><ul>
<li>Maintain a current herd or flock inventory. Record species, number, identification, photographs, usual location, ownership, veterinarian, and special handling or medical needs.</li>
<li>Map gates, pens, pastures, barns, water shutoffs, feed, handling equipment, and vehicle access. Give the map and access information to caregivers.</li>
<li>Keep species-appropriate halters, leads, panels, gates, cages, loading aids, and protective equipment ready and in working order.</li>
<li>Confirm enough suitable towing vehicles, trailers, drivers, handlers, fuel, and accessible loading space. Practice loading before an emergency; never exceed equipment ratings.</li>
<li>Set a loading order that accounts for temperament, compatibility, medical needs, young animals, and the time each group needs. Keep incompatible animals separated.</li>
<li>Confirm destinations with the facility and local authorities. Ask about capacity, identification, health records, biosecurity, feed and water, sanitation, unloading, and who provides daily care.</li>
</ul></div>

## If normal household transport will not work

<ol class="procedure">
<li><strong>Identify the gap now.</strong> Note animals that are too large, numerous, difficult to handle, medically fragile, or unable to use the available vehicle or carrier.</li>
<li><strong>Arrange qualified help.</strong> Make written primary and backup arrangements with trained caregivers, transporters, boarding facilities, neighbors, or mutual-aid partners. Confirm how they will gain access and who may authorize care.</li>
<li><strong>Set an early trigger.</strong> Decide when to call and load—before roads, air quality, weather, staffing, or animal behavior make movement unsafe. Reconfirm the plan during high-risk periods.</li>
<li><strong>Plan a locally approved alternative.</strong> Ask local emergency management, animal control, agricultural extension, and the veterinarian what to do if evacuation is impossible. Record only instructions verified for the location, species, and hazard; do not release animals or improvise an unsafe move.</li>
</ol>
